# parable

## 1. Pick the concept

- Argument names a concept → use it.
- Argument names a field → pick a graduate-level concept from it.
- No argument → pick a field and a concept yourself, and vary the choice between runs.

Graduate-level means a course past the undergraduate core would teach it: not "supply and demand" but "the winner's curse", not "entropy" but "the renormalization group". It needs a mechanism, a cause and an effect, or there is no plot to tell.

## 2. Write the fable

- Animals, villagers, kingdoms, artisans: a world with none of the field's vocabulary. No jargon, no names of theorems, nothing that gives the concept away early.
- The plot *is* the mechanism. Each turn of the story maps to a step of the concept, so a reader who follows the story has followed the idea.
- Withhold the reveal. The reader should only realize what the story is about near the end; a closing line or a moral can tip it, but never name the concept inside the story.
- Keep it tight: a few hundred to about a thousand words.

## 3. Explain the concept

After the story, under a separator:

- Name the concept and its field.
- Give the precise definition at graduate level, with the formula or formal statement when there is one.
- Map the story back onto it: which character, object, or event stands for which part.
- Say where the analogy breaks, since every analogy does.

Write in the language the user writes in.
